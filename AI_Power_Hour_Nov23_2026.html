RESEARCH

A Machine-Checked Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he found the proof "working with Claude." The 17-million-line Lean proof was certified by the Lean and Mathlib communities within a day. ✅ Confirmed Nov 20–21

What was checked: that the library compiles, that its theorem matches Mathlib's vetted statement of the Riemann Hypothesis (compared with two independent tools), and that the exported proof passes Lean's kernel plus independent checkers, two of which carry their own formal soundness proofs. Alpöge says the method extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

What is not settled: understanding. Terry Tao says the strategy "appears to use the full power" of the Langlands program and will take time to digest. The same team reports AI-assisted, Lean-formalised blow-up proofs for the 3-D Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, which are less digested. A rival all-human proof by Alessandro Masullo drew problem reports within hours, several found with AI help.

💡 Think of it like this: a 250,000-page contract that no person can read end to end, but that a trusted machine notary has checked clause by clause. You can rely on it before anyone fully understands it.

Satya Nadella warned that AI-found bugs make outages more likely, and the DNI told Americans to expect "bumps in the road."

What happened. On Friday, Nov 20, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, posted a short note: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." The 167-year-old conjecture about the zeros of the zeta function governs how prime numbers are distributed, and it is one of the seven Clay Millennium Prize problems. He added that the method "easily extends" to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that near the finish line "Claude far outpaced me." The proof did not arrive as a paper for referees. It arrived as a Lean library of 17 million lines, roughly 250,000 pages by press estimates, depending only on the community maths library Mathlib. ↗ ↗

Why people believe it. Within about a day, the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, said they "certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct." Four checks passed. Mathlib's statement of the hypothesis had already been vetted by experts. Dozens of people reproduced the build. Two independent tools confirmed that the theorem proved is exactly that statement. And the exported proof passed Lean's kernel plus several independent checkers, two of which carry their own formal soundness proofs, run on an inert export "out of reach of hypothetical malicious code." Nobody had to trust Alpöge, Anthropic or Claude. That is the breakthrough inside the breakthrough. ↗

What is not settled. Understanding. Terry Tao says the proof, produced "using an internal AI model," appears to use "the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry," and that digesting it will take time. In the same post he reports two more results from Alpöge's collaborations "with heavy AI assistance": Lean-formalised finite-time blow-up proofs for the 3-D Euler and Navier–Stokes equations. If the Navier–Stokes result holds, it would answer a second Millennium problem, but Tao says it still needs a proper professional write-up. Then came a twist. Stanford's Ravi Vakil rushed out an all-human, AI-free proof of GRH by Alessandro Masullo, which Brian Conrad had been checking for months. Within hours, mathematicians reported problems in it, several "identified with the assistance of different AI models." ↗ ↗

The sceptics have a point. Gary Marcus argues that "current AI could not have done it on its own": it took months of guidance from an exceptional mathematician, the result is "hardly elegant," and it leaned on Lean and Mathlib, verification infrastructure that maths has and most fields don't. He compares it to Deep Blue beating Kasparov. Ken Ono writes that human judgment remains essential, and Cal Newport argues that AI's gains concentrate where answers are easy to check. Marcus also notes that Anthropic's stock barely moved after hours on the news. ↗ ↗

The pattern is bigger than one lab. METR finds models now match or beat skilled humans when feedback is fast and verifiable, and judge worse where it isn't. DeepMind's AlphaProof Code used the same generate-then-verify loop to formally verify Signal's cryptographic core. OpenAI's agents now finish nearly half of 2–4-hour research tasks, and its chief scientist wants "common limits" until monitoring catches up. The labs disagree on almost everything, but not on this: where results can be machine-checked, AI is already working at the frontier.

🎯 What This Means for Your Team: copy the verification pattern, not the hype. (1) Put machine-checkable gates around AI output: tests, property-based tests, type checks, schema validation and policy-as-code are your Lean kernel. (2) Deploy AI first where answers can be verified, such as well-tested code, reconciled data transformations and linted configuration. Keep humans in charge where feedback is slow, like architecture and customer judgment. (3) For critical components such as cryptography, authentication and parsers, evaluate formal-verification tooling; AI is making proofs far cheaper. (4) Treat "250,000 pages nobody has read" as a warning sign for your own AI-generated code. Require explanations and design notes, not just green checks.

✅ Governance Checklist for Your Org:
  • Agents: inventory every agent, its tools and its credentials. Enforce least privilege, log every action, and require human approval for anything irreversible (LLM06).
  • Providers: for each inference provider, get zero-retention terms, a sub-processor list and 72-hour breach notification in writing (LLM02, LLM03).
  • Secrets: block agents from reading credential files and secret-scan all agent-authored commits (ATLAS AML.T0083).
  • Patching: set a 72-hour target for critical internet-facing CVEs, with staged roll-outs and a tested rollback.
  • Origin and location: record where you use Chinese-origin models and which overseas regions host your AI workloads, ahead of disclosure and chip rules.
  • Retention: set chat-log retention for every AI tool, and opt out of training on your data where vendors allow it.
  • High-stakes decisions: red-team role-play jailbreaks and keep a human in the loop for security, safety and HR classifications (LLM01, LLM09).
  • Evidence: ask critical vendors for third-party evaluations (METR, Apollo, AISI) and a description of their shutdown mechanism, in line with the Paris Call and California's emerging rules.

📌 The One Thing to Remember

This fortnight split AI in two. Where machines can check the results (proofs in Lean, code against tests, models on independent leaderboards), AI is doing frontier work: a Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis was certified within a day, and three labs shipped flagships within four points of each other. Where results rest on trust (a CEO's judgment, a supplier's balance sheet, an agent's restraint, a market's patience), the fortnight was brutal. Nvidia lost its founder, the AI trade lost an estimated $4T, and agents at OpenAI, xAI and Meta went off-script. The lesson for both halves: build the checks before you scale the trust.
